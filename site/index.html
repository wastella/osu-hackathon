Stress is the loudest signal

Exercise barely registers

Plot daily activity minutes against sleep quality and the trend line is nearly flat. The most active people (90 min/day) sleep no better on average than people who move 30 minutes. Daily steps show even less: r = +0.02.

Activity & sleep, again4/9

The flat line hides two opposite stories

Split by occupation and the slope comes back. Among doctors, more activity goes with much better sleep. Among engineers, it goes with worse sleep. Averaged together, the two cancel out. This is Simpson's paradox: a trend that appears inside groups can vanish or reverse when the groups are pooled.

Each job forms its own constellation

Color the stars by sleep disorder and the occupations separate sharply. 84% of nurses have sleep apnea. 91% of salespeople and 68% of teachers have insomnia. Engineers are the calmest group: lowest stress (3.9), best quality (8.4), and 90% with no disorder.

Body & disorders6/9

But BMI sorts them even better

Regroup the same stars by BMI category. 93% of normal-BMI people have no disorder. Among overweight people, 87% have one, and every obese person does. Blood pressure tells a similar story: of the 71 people with systolic pressure of 140 or higher, 86% have sleep apnea.

So is it the job, or the body? In this data you can't separate them. All 73 nurses are women, their average age is 52, and 90% are overweight.

Checking the data8/9

374 stars, 132 people

Group identical rows and the sky collapses. Only 132 combinations of values are unique. One profile, a 37-year-old female accountant sleeping 7.2 hours, appears 13 times. Person IDs are also sorted perfectly by age, and nobody is younger than 27 or older than 59.

Everything shown so far still holds on the 132 unique profiles, but the duplicates inflate how confident it looks. This dataset is best treated as illustrative, not as a sample of real people.

Thinking critically

What the data can and can't tell us

A pattern in a dataset is not a cause. Here is where we draw the line on each finding.

SupportedWithin this dataset

  • Higher stress goes with shorter, worse sleep.r = −0.90 with quality and −0.81 with hours, and −0.88 on the 132 unique profiles. It holds inside every occupation too (−0.75 to −0.98).
  • Overweight and obese BMI go with sleep disorders.88% of the 158 overweight or obese rows have insomnia or apnea, against 7% of normal-BMI rows.
  • The activity–sleep link depends on the group.Pooled r = +0.19, but +0.85 for doctors, +0.96 for accountants and −0.54 for engineers.
  • Occupations differ sharply in disorders.Apnea dominates nurses (84%), insomnia dominates salespeople (91%) and teachers (68%).

Not supportedClaims we avoid

  • "Stress causes poor sleep."The arrow could point the other way (bad sleep raises stress), or a third factor like workload could drive both. The data was collected at a single point in time, so it can't show order.
  • "Nursing causes sleep apnea."Occupation is tangled with gender, age and BMI: every nurse is a woman and 90% are overweight. Job and body can't be separated here.
  • "Exercise doesn't matter for sleep."A flat pooled line hides real within-group slopes, and activity here is a single self-reported number.
  • "These rates describe real workers."65% of rows are duplicates, IDs are sorted by age, and the subgroups split too cleanly. The dataset looks generated, so its patterns describe the dataset, not the population.
Method

Data: Sleep Health and Lifestyle Dataset (Kaggle), 374 rows. "Normal Weight" was merged into "Normal" for BMI. Correlations are Pearson r on all rows unless noted; "unique profiles" means rows that match on every column except Person ID. Disorder shares are row counts within each group. Every chart on this page is computed live from the embedded CSV; the same figures can be reproduced with the Python scripts in the project's analysis/ folder.
